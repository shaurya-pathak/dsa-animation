# Prime Brokerage — Layperson Story Contract

## Intent translation and learning transformation

This video is for someone beginning a role in finance who has heard the term
**prime brokerage** but cannot yet explain why it exists.

**Goal:** Build a usable layperson mental model of the problem prime brokerage
solves, rather than asking the viewer to memorize a service list.

- **Before:** “A prime broker sounds like a more important version of a normal
  broker.”
- **After:** “A fund can execute trades through several brokers, while a prime
  broker provides the central operating and financing relationship that makes
  accepted trades manageable afterward.”

The video must work as an explanation with the sound off and as spoken English
with the visuals hidden. It must establish the problem before naming the
solution. It must not begin with a trade ticket, a status badge, or a list of
services.

## Viewer question and promise

**Viewer question:** Why would a fund trade through several brokers but still
need a prime broker?

**Promise:** We will first make the post-trade problem visible, then use one
analogy and one purchase to show exactly what the prime relationship adds.

An investment fund may trade through several executing brokers. One may find a
better price for one order; another may be better suited to a different order.
But getting a trade agreed is only the beginning of the operational work.

After each trade:

- money must be paid or received;
- securities must be delivered or received;
- a short position may need borrowed shares;
- some positions may need financing;
- the fund needs a reliable record of what this prime relationship carries,
  owes, and has borrowed.

If every trade remained in a separate operational pile at the broker that
executed it, the fund would have to reconcile cash, positions, loans, and risk
across all of those piles. Prime brokerage exists to give an institutional
fund a central relationship for the accepted trades that relationship carries.

## Audience starting point

The viewer understands that funds buy and sell investments but may treat
**broker**, **prime broker**, **fill**, **settlement**, **short**, and **margin**
as interchangeable jargon. The video assumes no knowledge of market plumbing.
It is scoped to a simplified institutional cash-equities relationship.

## Mental model and analogy map

Think of executing brokers as different storefronts and the prime broker as a
central receiving warehouse with a secured credit desk.

- The fund chooses what to order.
- A storefront completes the purchase.
- The warehouse accepts eligible deliveries from several storefronts and keeps
  one inventory record for what arrives at that warehouse.
- The credit desk may lend against eligible inventory.
- If an item is being sold before the fund owns it, a lending arrangement must
  supply that borrowed item.

The analogy is only a bridge. Securities move electronically through market
infrastructure, the prime broker may reject a trade that falls outside the
agreement, and contractual and regulatory responsibilities are more complex
than receiving a parcel.

| Everyday element | Machine or financial-system meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Several storefronts | Several executing brokers |
| One receiving warehouse | One prime-broker relationship for accepted trades it carries |
| Inventory arriving at the warehouse | Positions resulting from settled trades |
| A bill and secured credit desk | Cash, collateral, and agreed financing |

## The definition the viewer should remember

**Prime brokerage is the central operating and financing relationship that
helps an institutional fund turn trades made through different executing
brokers into positions it can settle, carry, finance, borrow for, report, and
monitor.**

The prime broker does not decide what the fund should buy or sell. It may not
be the broker that executes a trade. If the fund uses several prime brokers,
one prime broker does not automatically see the whole portfolio.

## Role boundaries

| Actor | One plain-English job | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Investment fund | Chooses the investment | Owns the investment decision and risk |
| Executing broker | Finds the other side and agrees the trade | A fill is an agreement, not yet the exchange of cash and securities |
| Prime broker | Accepts eligible trades into the relationship and operates the resulting account | Decides what it will carry and finance; does not choose the investment |
| Settlement infrastructure | Completes the exchange of cash and securities | It is not the investment adviser or the fund's operating account |
| Stock-loan arrangement | Supplies and maintains borrowed shares for a short | Borrow availability is not guaranteed and can change |

## Training, prediction, and outcome boundary

- **Earlier:** the fund, prime broker, and approved executing
  brokers have agreements, accounts, operating connections, and limits.
- **Now:** the fund chooses an investment and sends an order.
- **Trade outcome:** an executing broker agrees a price and quantity.
- **After:** the prime broker accepts an eligible trade into this
  relationship; market infrastructure completes settlement; the prime account
  records and carries the resulting position.
- **Never imply:** the prime broker chose the investment, every trade is
  automatically accepted, or one prime broker sees positions held elsewhere.

## Concrete entry point

Use one fictional example and explain why it exists before showing any status.

The fund wants to own 100 shares of a fictional company called Northstar.
Northstar has no special meaning; it is simply one purchase we can follow from
decision to completed position.

The same large card remains visible throughout the sequence:

1. **The fund decides:** “Buy 100 Northstar.” This is only an instruction.
2. **Broker A executes:** Broker A finds a seller and agrees the quantity and
   price. People call this a **fill**. A fill means the trade has been agreed;
   it does not mean the shares have arrived.
3. **The prime broker checks the trade:** The trade details reach the prime
   broker. It checks that the record matches and that the trade fits the
   account's agreed operating and credit limits. If it accepts the trade, it
   will coordinate and record the next stage within this relationship.
4. **The trade settles:** Through market infrastructure, cash moves out and
   100 Northstar shares move in. This exchange is **settlement**.
5. **A position now exists in the account:** The account records
   “+100 Northstar.” This position did not appear from nowhere; it is the result
   of the Northstar purchase the viewer has watched from the beginning.

The payoff is stated immediately: Broker A executed the trade, but the fund's
operations team can now manage the resulting position, cash, and records in
the prime-broker relationship. Trades executed through other approved brokers
can arrive at the same operating relationship.

## What the prime relationship adds

Only after the Northstar example is complete should the video add two services.

### Borrowing for a short

Suppose the fund expects the price of another fictional company, Harbor, to
fall. It wants to sell 40 Harbor shares now and buy them back later. Because it
does not own those shares, it needs to borrow them.

Broker B must determine before execution that shares can be borrowed for
delivery. An actual stock-loan arrangement then supports the short while it
remains open, often through the prime broker's stock-loan desk or lending
partners. The account must always show **Short 40 Harbor** beside
**Borrowed 40 Harbor**. Never show one without the other.

### Financing and margin

The prime broker may lend the fund part of the money used to support its
positions. This is a secured loan, not free buying power. The fund supplies
capital and collateral; the prime broker monitors the cushion protecting the
loan.

Use one causal example: Harbor's price rises, so the short loses money. That
loss shrinks the collateral cushion. The prime broker may then require more
collateral or less exposure. Do not teach a made-up universal margin
percentage.

## Spoken narration

### Beat 1 — Why there is a problem

Imagine you help operate an investment fund. The fund buys and sells hundreds
of investments, and it does not always trade through the same broker. Broker A
might find the best price for one order. Broker B might be better for another.

That flexibility is useful. But it creates a problem after the trade. Money
still has to move. Securities still have to arrive. A short may need borrowed
shares. Some positions may need financing. And the fund needs a reliable record
of what this prime relationship carries.

If every trade stayed in a separate pile at the broker that executed it, the
operations team would spend its day piecing those piles together. The fund
needs one central relationship for the accepted trades it carries.

### Beat 2 — The mental model

Think of Broker A and Broker B as two different storefronts. The fund can shop
at either one. But after it buys something, it wants the accepted purchase sent
to one receiving warehouse, where the inventory and the bill can be managed
together.

That receiving warehouse is the useful mental model for a prime broker. The
analogy is not exact—securities move electronically, not in boxes—but it gives
us the central idea. Executing brokers help the fund make trades. A prime broker
helps the fund operate and finance the accepted positions that result from
those trades.

### Beat 3 — Follow one purchase

Let's follow one purchase slowly. The fund decides to buy 100 shares of a
fictional company called Northstar. Northstar is just an example we can keep
our eyes on from beginning to end.

First, the fund sends the order to Broker A. Broker A finds a seller, and they
agree on the quantity and price. In market language, the order has been filled.
That word only means the trade has been agreed. The fund does not have the
shares yet.

Next, the Northstar trade record reaches the prime broker. The prime broker
checks that the details match and that the trade fits the limits already agreed
for this account. If the trade is accepted, the prime broker coordinates and
records what happens next.

Now the trade settles. Through market infrastructure, the fund's cash moves
out and 100 Northstar shares move in. Only now does the account show a completed
position: plus 100 Northstar.

Northstar did not appear from nowhere. It is the same purchase we have followed
the entire time: the fund chose it, Broker A executed it, the prime broker
accepted it into the relationship, and settlement exchanged cash for shares.

This is why the fund cares. Broker A may have executed the trade, but the
resulting position, cash, and records can now be operated through the fund's
prime-broker account. Another approved broker can execute a different trade
and send its accepted result to that same relationship.

### Beat 4 — A short needs borrowed shares

Now suppose the fund believes Harbor will fall. It wants to sell 40 Harbor
shares now and buy them back later. But the fund does not own those shares, so
it cannot simply pretend they exist. It needs to borrow them.

Before Broker B executes the short, it must determine that shares can be
borrowed for delivery. Then a stock-loan arrangement supports that borrowing
while the short stays open, often through the prime broker or its lending
partners.

That is why the prime account shows two facts together: short 40 Harbor, and
borrowed 40 Harbor. The short can remain open only while the borrow is
available.

### Beat 5 — Financing creates a margin obligation

The prime broker may also lend the fund part of the money supporting its
positions. That is secured financing. The fund provides capital and collateral,
and the prime broker monitors the cushion protecting the loan.

Suppose Harbor rises instead of falling. The short loses money, so that cushion
shrinks. The prime broker may ask the fund to add collateral or reduce the
position. The exact rules vary, but the reason is simple: the loan must remain
protected as the value of the account changes.

### Beat 6 — The complete mental model

Prime brokerage is not a smarter version of an ordinary broker. It is the
central operating and financing relationship behind an institutional fund's
trading.

The fund chooses the investments. Executing brokers make the trades. Market
infrastructure completes the exchange. Stock lending supports borrowed shares.
And the prime broker accepts eligible trades into the relationship, carries the
positions, provides agreed financing and reporting, and monitors collateral.

Several trading doors. One place to operate the accepted positions. That is
the problem prime brokerage solves.

## Confusion traps to prevent

- Do not introduce Northstar before saying it is one fictional purchase the
  viewer will follow.
- Do not use standalone badges such as **FILLED**, **MATCH + LIMITS**, or
  **ACCEPTED** without translating the action into ordinary English.
- Do not point an arrow at an actor when the intended meaning is a change in
  the trade's state.
- Do not show a position before cash and securities exchange.
- Do not show **Short 40 Harbor** without **Borrowed 40 Harbor**.
- Do not show more than one unfamiliar mechanism in the focal area.
- Do not leave chapter boundaries on authored timestamps after narration is
  generated; the measured voice duration is authoritative.

## Visual contract

- Begin with one large fund and two large broker storefronts. Do not show the
  words “prime broker” until the scattered post-trade obligations are visible.
- Introduce the warehouse analogy as a transformation of those scattered piles
  into one large receiving destination.
- During the Northstar sequence, keep one large **BUY 100 NORTHSTAR** card on
  screen. Add numbered verbs around it; do not replace it with unexplained
  badges such as **FILLED**, **MATCH + LIMITS**, or **ACCEPTED**.
- Show **fill** with the plain-English subtitle **TRADE AGREED**.
- Show **settlement** as a literal exchange: cash moves one way and shares move
  the other.
- Transform the same Northstar trade card into **+100 NORTHSTAR IN ACCOUNT**.
  Never introduce the position in a new composition.
- Introduce no more than one unfamiliar mechanism at a time. Use at most one
  sentence of on-screen supporting text.
- Make the focal object occupy roughly one third of the frame. Every label must
  remain readable on a laptop without zooming.
- Do not use glow. Use motion only to show a causal action or preserve object
  identity.
- Do not cut or fade a visual chapter until the narration for that chapter has
  finished. Generate the selected voice first, map cues against the resulting
  audio, and render only from the retimed artifact.

## Acceptance checks before render

Before rendering, a layperson must be able to answer these questions from the
written story alone:

- **Sound-off:** the causal path remains understandable with narration muted.
- **Teach-back:** the viewer can explain the executing broker and prime broker
  using different verbs.
- **Counterfactual:** before the answer appears, the viewer predicts that a
  Harbor loss shrinks the cushion and can require collateral or less exposure.

1. What problem exists after a fund trades through several brokers?
2. How is an executing broker different from a prime broker?
3. What does “filled” mean, and what has not happened yet?
4. Where did the +100 Northstar position come from?
5. Why does a short position need borrowed shares?
6. Why can a loss lead to a request for collateral?

If any answer requires outside knowledge, the story is not ready to animate.
